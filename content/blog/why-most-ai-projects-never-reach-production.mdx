Every year, enterprises pour billions into AI initiatives. Most of those initiatives never see the light of production. They die in POC purgatory, get shelved after a demo that wowed the boardroom, or quietly fail six months after launch when nobody's measuring the right things.

The Gartner estimate has held steady for years: **roughly 70% of AI and machine learning projects fail before they create business value.**

At LaunchNex, we were built specifically to fix this. But first, let's understand *why* it happens.

## The Four Root Causes

### 1. Building Before Assessing

The most common failure pattern starts before a single line of code is written. A stakeholder sees a competitor using AI and mandates a project. A vendor demos a flashy prototype. Everyone gets excited and signs the contract.

Nobody asks the hard question: **Is AI actually the right tool for this problem?**

AI is powerful, but it's not magic. It requires the right data, the right problem structure, and the right business context. Skipping feasibility assessment is like building a house without checking if the land can support it.

We spend the first two weeks of every engagement on exactly this — what we call the **Clarity Sprint**. If AI won't help, we say so before you've spent anything on development.

### 2. Junior Engineers on Critical Work

Cost-cutting in AI projects is a false economy. AI engineering requires deep expertise in:

- ML system design and model selection
- Data pipeline architecture
- Evaluation frameworks and hallucination mitigation
- Production monitoring and drift detection

When these decisions are made by engineers without production AI experience, the technical debt accumulates fast. What looks like a working system in staging turns into a fragile, unmaintainable liability in production.

Every engagement at LaunchNex is led by senior engineers. Not mentored by them — *led* by them.

### 3. No Definition of "Done"

Most AI projects lack contractual SLAs on the metrics that actually matter:

- Accuracy or F1 score thresholds
- Latency budgets
- Hallucination rate limits
- Fallback behavior under uncertainty

Without these, "done" means whatever the vendor says it means. You take delivery of something that looks finished but has no performance guarantees — and you find out it doesn't work at the worst possible time.

### 4. The Post-Launch Cliff

Even projects that reach production often fail quietly afterward. AI systems degrade over time as data distributions shift. The model that worked perfectly in January starts giving wrong answers by June, and nobody notices until a customer complains.

This is **model drift** — and it requires proactive monitoring to catch.

Most vendors deliver the system and disappear. We stay for 90 days post-launch with automated monitoring and drift detection built into every engagement.

## The Pattern of Projects That Succeed

Looking at the 30% that make it, some consistent patterns emerge:

**They start with constraints, not ambitions.** The successful projects define what "AI won't do" before defining what it will. They have a clear fallback when the model is uncertain.

**They measure the right things.** Business KPIs — not just model accuracy. Reduced support tickets, faster triage time, higher conversion. The engineering metrics serve those business metrics.

**They treat data as infrastructure.** Not as something to figure out later. Data quality, lineage, and governance are first-class engineering concerns from day one.

**They ship incrementally.** Not a big-bang deployment but a phased rollout with feedback loops at each stage.

## What This Means for Your Next AI Project

Before you start, answer these questions honestly:

1. Do we have the data to train or fine-tune a model — or are we assuming it exists?
2. What does "good enough" performance look like, in specific, measurable terms?
3. Who owns this system in production? Who is responsible when it breaks?
4. What happens to users when the AI is wrong or unavailable?

If you can't answer these, you're not ready to build. You're ready to assess.

That's not a failure — it's the most valuable thing you can do before committing budget and engineering time.
